Why your easy pace must slow down

Your natural easy pace of 6:00/km is only 24 sec/km slower than your marathon goal. That gap is too small — every easy day is actually a moderate effort, leaving you under-recovered and grinding the IT band down. Running at 6:45–7:15 feels embarrassingly slow for the first week. That is exactly right.

How to check pace on Strava without a watch

Run with your phone in a pocket or running armband. Strava uses GPS to calculate pace automatically. After each run, check your average pace and the pace graph — easy runs should read 6:45–7:20, long runs 7:00–7:30, tempo segments clearly faster in the split view.

Before every run — 10 min
Clamshells
3 × 15 each side · resistance band around knees
Lie on your side, feet together, band around knees. Rotate the top knee upward like a clamshell opening, keeping feet touching. Lower slowly. This activates the glute medius — the root cause of IT band issues when weak.
Side-lying hip abduction
3 × 15 each side · no equipment needed
Lie on your side, bottom leg bent for balance, top leg straight. Lift the top leg to roughly 45° and lower slowly — do not let it drop. Strengthens hip abductors which stabilise the knee under load.
Single-leg glute bridge
3 × 12 each side · hold 2 sec at top
Lie on your back, one knee bent with foot flat on the floor, other leg straight and lifted. Drive through the planted heel to raise hips until your body forms a straight line. Prevents hip drop during the run, which is what loads the IT band.
Hip flexor stretch — kneeling lunge
60 sec each side
Kneel on one knee, other foot forward. Push hips forward gently until you feel a deep stretch in the front of the back hip. Keep upright. Tightness here tilts the pelvis and directly stresses the IT band.
TFL and glute foam roll
60 sec each side · foam roller
Roll the outer hip area — the TFL (just below the hip bone) and the glutes. Never roll directly on the IT band on the outer thigh itself — it is a tendon and rolling it causes irritation. Roll above and below only.
Evening physio session — 15 min, on non-run days
Monster walks
3 sets × 15 steps each direction · band around ankles
Band around ankles, slight squat position. Walk sideways keeping toes forward and tension in the band. Builds hip stability under dynamic load — the kind needed at km 30.
Step-downs (eccentric)
3 × 12 each side · use a step or low stair
Stand on a step on one leg. Slowly lower the opposite heel toward the floor over 3 seconds. Return. The slow eccentric phase is what builds the protective quad and glute strength that shields the knee.
IT band stretch — standing cross-leg
3 × 45 sec each side
Stand, cross the affected (left) leg behind the other. Lean sideways away from the crossed leg — lean right to stretch the left IT band — until you feel a pull on the outer thigh. Hold steady.
Pigeon pose
90 sec each side · on a yoga mat
From all fours, bring one knee forward and angle it outward, extend the other leg straight back. Sink the hips toward the floor. This releases the piriformis and deep hip rotators that pull on the IT band when tight.
Lateral band walks
3 × 20 steps each direction · band around ankles
Band around ankles, feet hip-width, slight bend in knees. Step sideways keeping toes pointing forward. Builds hip abductor endurance — important because it is the fatigue at km 25+ that causes the IT band to flare.
Single-leg calf raises
3 × 15 each side · on a step edge
Stand on the ball of one foot on a step edge, heel hanging off. Lower the heel below the step level slowly, then rise up onto toes. Reduces lower leg fatigue on the run and protects the full knee chain.
Tightness vs pain — know the line. Tightness or mild awareness of the left outer knee = continue running, slow your pace, monitor. A sharp, stabbing sensation on the outer knee — especially going downhill — = stop immediately. Walk home. Ice 15 min. Do the evening physio. Reassess the next day.

Race pacing strategy
km 0–5
6:35–6:45 /km Deliberately slower than goal pace. Let people pass you. The adrenaline will make this feel too slow — that is correct.
km 5–20
6:20–6:30 /km Settle into race pace. Find a rhythm. Keep hips level, activate glutes consciously. Short strides, high cadence — aim for ~175 steps/min.
km 20–30
6:20–6:30 /km Hold form. Walk 90 sec every 5 km as planned — this costs you nothing and saves the knee. Take your second gel around km 22.
km 30–35
6:30–7:00 /km IT band most likely to flare here. If pain arrives: slow to 7:00, walk 90 sec every 3 km. Walk the downhills. This costs 3 minutes — not 30. If taking ibuprofen, take it now with a gel.
km 35–42.2
Run on feel. Walk what you need. Run the tangents — avoid cambered (tilted) roads which stress the outer knee. Finish strong.
On pain killers

If you take ibuprofen mid-race, take it at km 30 with a gel or food — never on an empty stomach during hard effort. It masks pain signals, so stay conscious of form: keep cadence high, glutes active, hips level. You have already made this decision — execute it well and finish the race.

After the finish

Ice both knees immediately. Elevate legs. Eat and drink within 30 min. No running for a minimum of 2–3 weeks post-race.
